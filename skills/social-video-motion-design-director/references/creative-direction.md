# Creative direction playbook

## Brief to viewer journey

Identify audience and viewing situation; what they know; the problem, question or emotion they care about; the core promise; and the outcome or payoff. For long-form, test whether the title/thumbnail's actual promise is delivered. For feed video, consider whether an unfamiliar viewer can orient themselves quickly; do not mandate one universal number of seconds. For ads, follow the specific campaign/placement and include a CTA only if the objective or brief requires it. A loop is optional.

## Visual proof before polish

For each beat ask:
1. What is the one main thing to understand or feel?
2. What information must be established first? What is its visible/audible proof?
3. Can an unfamiliar viewer connect that proof with the stated conclusion? Are order, causality, units and context explicit where they matter?
4. What is the focal point, and is competing detail reduced appropriately?
5. Is the idea advancing, or is this a decorative restatement?
6. Can it be understood at the intended size and with the planned audio/caption experience?

Example: showing three items leave a box in the order 1, 2, 3 does not by itself prove first-in-first-out. Show or explicitly establish arrival order before showing the first item leave; indicate the simplified nature of a schematic if applicable. Before handing off, compare hook, narration, headings, captions, graphics, movement and payoff: do not overstate a source or let a diagram imply exclusivity, an exhaustive set, a real transaction, or quantitative data that was not established. Where a narrated factor is load-bearing, show or otherwise communicate it; ensure the visual's motion actually demonstrates the stated action.

## Hook and pacing

Produce distinct hook options only when useful, and ensure each is truthful and paid off. A quick change is not automatically more engaging than a hold. Map the progression of question, evidence, understanding, tension, relief or emotion; describe why each hold, transition or change helps. Look for openings that say something is occurring but show only a static label. Avoid arbitrary periodic interrupts and unearned cliffhangers.

## Visual and motion choices

- Pick a visual thesis suited to the subject: typography, diagram, real footage, interface, illustration, 2D, 3D or a mix. Do not default to an effect or an aesthetic on every project.
- Establish text hierarchy, relative size and contrast, comfortable margins and relationships to other elements. Test rendered text at intended display size after movement settles.
- Describe what the camera or object is doing, its start/end state and path, timing, easing, hold, and reason. Indicate what persists, resets, or is allowed to occlude another element.
- Check whether multiple moving and reading tasks compete for attention. Use intentional quiet as well as energy.
- Where the claim relies on real UI, charts or products, request real assets and data; mark illustrative diagrams clearly. Never manufacture a testimonial or fictional interface and present it as real.
- 3D direction should include narrative purpose, asset and camera needs, appearance, expected resources, and a practical 2D fallback where appropriate; it does not itself prove a 3D render is feasible.

## Sound and accessibility

Coordinate narration, music, SFX, silence and captions. Meaningful non-speech audio belongs in synchronized captions; descriptions of essential visual information may need audio description or a suitable alternative. Captions are not equivalent to decorative animated keywords. Consider muted viewing if relevant, but do not assume sound is absent from all platforms. Check rights and available assets; do not invent permissions.
